1. Enumerate the six colors: forest=0, olive=1, peach=2, turquoise=3, white=4, yellow=5.  
2. Represent rug configurations as sets of colors; since exactly five colors are used total, and each used color appears in exactly one rug, the three rugs partition a 5-element subset of the six colors.  
3. Since there are exactly two solid rugs, the third rug must be multicolored and contain the remaining 3 colors (since 2 solid rugs × 1 color + multicolored rug × k colors = total used colors = 5 ⇒ k=3).  
4. Model the possible solid rug color pairs as candidate sets: for each answer choice, assume those two colors are the solid rugs (i.e., each forms its own singleton rug), and check if a valid assignment for the third multicolored rug (using exactly 3 of the remaining 4 colors) exists satisfying all rules.  
5. Add constraints for each candidate pair:  
   - Let the two solid colors be s1 and s2 (must be distinct).  
   - The third rug R must consist of exactly 3 colors from the remaining 4 colors (i.e., all colors except s1, s2, and one excluded color).  
   - Enforce all rules on R:  
     a. If white ∈ R, then |R| ≥ 3 and two *other* colors must also be in R — but since |R|=3, this requires white ∈ R ⇒ R = {white, a, b} with a,b ≠ white — automatically satisfied as long as white ∈ R.  
     b. If olive ∈ R, then peach ∈ R.  
     c. Forest and turquoise cannot both be in R.  
     d. Peach and turquoise cannot both be in R.  
     e. Peach and yellow cannot both be in R.  
6. For each answer choice, create a solver instance, assert that s1 and s2 are solid rugs (i.e., each appears alone in its rug), assert the third rug R uses exactly 3 colors from the complement set, and enforce all above rules on R.  
7. If no model exists (UNSAT), then the solid rug pair is **impossible**, and thus belongs to the answer set.  
8. Collect all impossible solid rug color pairs and output their indices in the choice list.
